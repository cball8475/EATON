# Domain 8 — Training, Education, and Communication (11%)

~19 scored items — the third-largest domain and the one candidates most often under-study
because it feels like common sense. It isn't. The models have names, levels, and orders,
and the exam asks for them precisely.

---

## 1. Adult learning theory

**Andragogy — Malcolm Knowles' six assumptions about adult learners:**

1. **Need to know** — adults want to know *why* before they'll invest in learning
2. **Self-concept** — adults are self-directed and resent being treated like children
3. **Experience** — adults bring a deep reservoir of experience that is itself a learning
   resource (and sometimes a barrier, when it conflicts with the new material)
4. **Readiness to learn** — tied to their real-life roles and immediate problems
5. **Orientation to learning** — **problem-centered**, not subject-centered
6. **Motivation** — primarily **internal** (self-esteem, quality of life, job satisfaction)
   rather than external

**Pedagogy vs. andragogy** — pedagogy is teacher-directed, dependent-learner, subject-centered;
andragogy is learner-directed, experience-based, problem-centered. Safety training for adults
should look like the second.

**Practical consequences the exam wants:** tell them why it matters to *them*; use their
experience; use real plant scenarios and real incidents; make it immediately applicable;
respect them.

**Experiential learning — Kolb's cycle:** Concrete Experience → Reflective Observation →
Abstract Conceptualization → Active Experimentation → back to experience. It's a cycle, and
you can enter it at any point.

**Social learning theory (Bandura)** — people learn by observing others and the consequences
those others receive. Which is exactly why a supervisor who cuts a corner teaches more than
the training did.

## 2. Bloom's taxonomy

**Cognitive domain (revised, 2001 — verbs, lowest to highest):**

| Level | Verb | Example objective |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Remember** | List the four classes of fire extinguishers |
| 2 | **Understand** | Explain why Class D fires cannot be extinguished with water |
| 3 | **Apply** | Select the correct extinguisher for a given fire |
| 4 | **Analyze** | Compare two suppression options for a flammable liquid room |
| 5 | **Evaluate** | Judge whether the existing system meets NFPA 10 |
| 6 | **Create** | Design a fire protection plan for the new building |

(The original 1956 order used *Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis,
Evaluation* — Synthesis and Evaluation swapped in the revision. If the choices are nouns,
it's the original; if verbs, it's the revision.)

**Three domains:** **cognitive** (knowledge), **psychomotor** (skills — imitation,
manipulation, precision, articulation, naturalization), **affective** (attitudes/values —
receiving, responding, valuing, organizing, characterizing).

**Why it matters for safety training:** most compliance training targets *remember*, while
the job requires *apply*. A written test measures level 1–2; a **hands-on demonstration**
measures level 3+. When a question asks how to verify competency, the answer is
demonstration, not a quiz.

**Writing objectives — the ABCD model:** **A**udience, **B**ehavior (an observable verb),
**C**ondition, **D**egree/criterion. "Given a fire extinguisher and a simulated Class A fire,
the employee will demonstrate the PASS technique with no errors." Verbs like *understand*
and *know* are not measurable and are wrong answers on objective-writing questions.

## 3. Instructional design — ADDIE

| Phase | Activity |
|---|---|
| **Analyze** | Needs assessment, task analysis, audience analysis, gap analysis. **Determine whether training is even the right solution** |
| **Design** | Learning objectives, sequence, methods, assessment strategy |
| **Develop** | Build the materials, job aids, and assessments; pilot |
| **Implement** | Deliver; manage logistics; train the trainers |
| **Evaluate** | Formative (during) and summative (after); feed results back to Analyze |

**The most-tested point in this domain: training is only the right fix when the problem is a
knowledge or skill deficit.** If workers know how but don't do it, the problem is systems,
incentives, tools, staffing, supervision, or design — and more training won't touch it.
Mager and Pipe's classic question: "Could they do it if their life depended on it?" If yes,
it isn't a training problem.

**Needs analysis levels:** organizational (does this align with goals and is the environment
supportive?) → **task/job** (what does the job actually require?) → **person** (who needs
what?).

**Gap analysis:** required competency − current competency = the gap. **Baseline competency**
is established by testing/observing before training, which is also the only way to prove the
training did anything.

## 4. Training methods

| Method | Strengths | Weaknesses | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Instructor-led classroom** | Interaction, Q&A, adaptable, group discussion | Cost, scheduling, consistency varies by instructor | Complex concepts, discussion, culture |
| **Computer-based / e-learning** | Consistent, scalable, self-paced, tracked, cheap per head | No hands-on, low engagement, click-through gaming | Awareness, refreshers, regulatory recall |
| **On-the-job training** | Real equipment, real context, immediate application | Only as good as the trainer; propagates bad habits; production pressure | Skills, procedures, equipment |
| **Hands-on / simulation** | Highest retention and transfer; safe failure | Cost, time, equipment | High-consequence skills — LOTO, confined space, extinguishers |
| **VR/AR** | Immersive, repeatable, safe, engaging | Cost, motion sickness, novelty effect | Rare high-consequence events, emergency response |
| **Toolbox talks / tailgate** | Frequent, short, job-specific, supervisor-delivered | Shallow, easily perfunctory | Reinforcement, daily hazards |
| **Group meeting / discussion** | Surfaces real conditions, builds ownership | Can drift, needs facilitation | Incident learning, culture |
| **Mentoring / coaching** | Individualized, sustained | Labor-intensive, mentor-dependent | New hires, high-risk roles |
| **Job aids** | Point-of-use, no memory load | Not training | Infrequent complex tasks — checklists, placards |

**Blended learning** — e-learning for the knowledge, hands-on for the skill, on-the-job for
the transfer — is generally the strongest answer when it's offered.

**Retention drivers:** spaced practice beats massed practice (cramming); **retrieval
practice** (testing yourself) beats re-reading; **practice to fluency**, not to first
success; and immediate feedback. Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve is the reason refreshers
exist — most of a single unreinforced session is lost within weeks.

## 5. Evaluating training effectiveness

**Kirkpatrick's four levels** — memorize the order and what each measures:

| Level | Name | Measures | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Reaction** | Did they like it? | Smile sheets, surveys |
| **2** | **Learning** | Did they acquire the knowledge/skill? | Pre/post tests, demonstrations |
| **3** | **Behavior** | Are they doing it on the job? | Observation, audits, supervisor reports — **weeks to months later** |
| **4** | **Results** | Did organizational outcomes change? | Incident rates, quality, cost, productivity |

**Phillips added Level 5: ROI** — `ROI (%) = [(Program benefits − Program costs) / Program
costs] × 100`.

**The whole point of the model is that Level 1 predicts almost nothing.** A well-liked class
that changes no behavior is a failed class. **Level 3 is where training actually pays off,
and it's the level almost nobody measures.** Expect a question that hands you a satisfaction
score and asks what it proves — the answer is "very little."

**Transfer of training** depends more on the work environment than on the class:
supervisor support, opportunity to apply, tools and time available, and consequences for
using or not using it. If the supervisor undercuts it, the training doesn't stick.

**Reaction/learning are easy and cheap; behavior/results are hard and meaningful.** The
difficulty and the value both increase up the levels.

## 6. OSHA training requirements and competency

**Standards with explicit training requirements** (a partial but exam-relevant list):

| Standard | Frequency |
|---|---|
| HazCom 1910.1200 | At initial assignment **and whenever a new hazard is introduced** |
| Bloodborne pathogens 1910.1030 | At assignment and **annually** |
| Respiratory protection 1910.134 | Before use and **annually** |
| Hearing conservation 1910.95 | **Annually** for employees in the program |
| Lockout/tagout 1910.147 | At assignment; retraining when jobs, machines, or procedures change, or when the periodic inspection finds deficiencies |
| Powered industrial trucks 1910.178 | Evaluation **every 3 years**; refresher after an incident, near miss, unsafe operation, new truck, or changed conditions |
| Fire extinguishers 1910.157 | At initial assignment and **annually** (if employees are expected to use them) |
| Confined space 1910.146 | Before assignment, when duties change, when hazards change, or when deviations are found |
| Fall protection 1926.503 | By a **competent person**; retraining when there's reason to believe the employee lacks understanding |
| HAZWOPER 1910.120 | Level-dependent; **8-hour annual refresher** |
| PSM 1910.119 | Refresher **at least every 3 years** |

**Training must be in a language and vocabulary the employee understands** — OSHA has
enforced this explicitly since 2010. Delivering English-only training to a workforce that
doesn't read English is a citable failure regardless of the sign-in sheet.

**Documentation:** who was trained, on what, by whom, when, and how competency was verified.
The sign-in sheet is the weakest form of evidence; a demonstrated-competency record is the
strongest.

### Competent vs. qualified vs. authorized

| Term | OSHA definition | Key |
|---|---|---|
| **Competent person** | One who is **capable of identifying existing and predictable hazards** in the surroundings or working conditions that are unsanitary, hazardous, or dangerous, **and who has authorization to take prompt corrective measures to eliminate them** | Two parts: **recognition + authority**. Missing the authority half is the most common wrong answer |
| **Qualified person** | One who, **by possession of a recognized degree, certificate, or professional standing, or by extensive knowledge, training, and experience**, has successfully demonstrated the ability to solve or resolve problems relating to the subject matter, the work, or the project | Credential or demonstrated expertise. Design work requires *qualified*, not *competent* |
| **Authorized person** | A person approved or assigned by the employer to perform a specific type of duty or to be at a specific location | Assignment, not necessarily expertise |
| **Qualified person (NFPA 70E)** | Has the skills and knowledge of construction and operation of the equipment **and** has received safety training to identify and avoid the hazards | Task- and equipment-specific |

**Where each is required:** competent person — excavations (daily inspection), scaffolds
(erection and inspection), fall protection training, ladders, confined-space evaluation in
construction, rigging inspection. Qualified person — designing scaffolds, engineering fall
protection anchorages, designing an excavation protective system over 20 ft (a **registered
professional engineer** specifically), electrical work under 70E.

## 7. Safety culture and climate

**Safety culture** = the shared values, beliefs, and assumptions about safety — deep,
slow-changing, and inferred. **Safety climate** = the *measurable* surface perception at a
point in time; the leading indicator of culture, assessed by survey. **Climate is the
snapshot; culture is the film.**

**DuPont Bradley Curve** — the four-stage maturity model:

| Stage | Mindset | Injury rate |
|---|---|---|
| **Reactive** | Safety is instinct; people react after incidents; "accidents happen" | Highest |
| **Dependent** | Safety is by compliance and supervision; rules, discipline, management-driven | Lower |
| **Independent** | Safety is a personal value; individuals take care of themselves | Lower still |
| **Interdependent** | Team ownership; people look out for each other; interventions are welcomed | Lowest |

**Interdependent is the goal.** Most organizations plateau at dependent, and the movement
from dependent to independent to interdependent requires trust, not more rules.

**Key elements of a positive culture:**

- **Leadership commitment demonstrated by behavior**, not statements. What leaders pay
  attention to, reward, and tolerate defines the real standard. Walking past a hazard sets
  it as acceptable
- **Employee participation** in hazard identification, investigation, and decisions
- **Psychological safety** (Amy Edmondson) — the shared belief that the team is safe for
  interpersonal risk-taking. Without it people don't report near misses, admit errors, or
  raise concerns, and your leading indicators go dark
- **Just culture** — distinguishing **human error** (console and fix the system) from
  **at-risk behavior** (coach, and understand why the drift happened) from **reckless
  behavior** (discipline). Punishing error destroys reporting; excusing recklessness
  destroys accountability. **Both extremes fail**
- **Open, two-way communication** and visible **closing of the loop** — reporters must see
  what happened to their report, or reporting stops
- **Trust** — the foundation, and the thing an unfair discipline decision destroys instantly
- **Accountability at every level**, including management
- **Learning orientation** — treating incidents and near misses as information, not failure

**Reporting rates are a paradox worth understanding:** a rising near-miss reporting rate
usually means the *culture* is improving, not that the workplace got more dangerous. Programs
that reward low injury numbers with prizes create **underreporting** — which is why OSHA's
anti-retaliation rule (1904.35/1904.36) targets incentive programs and blanket post-incident
drug testing that would deter reporting.

**Emotional intelligence (Goleman)** — self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy,
social skill. The competency behind influencing without authority, which is most of what a
safety professional does.

**Behavior-based safety (BBS):** built on the **ABC model** — **A**ntecedent (what triggers
the behavior), **B**ehavior, **C**onsequence (what follows and therefore drives repetition).
Consequences that are **Soon, Certain, and Positive** shape behavior far more strongly than
those that are late, uncertain, and negative — which is exactly why shortcuts win over
safety (immediate certain reward vs. delayed uncertain injury).

BBS elements: observation by peers, feedback, data collection, and involvement of workers in
defining the behaviors. **The critique** — and the exam knows it — is that BBS focuses
downstream on the worker's behavior when the upstream conditions (design, staffing, tooling,
schedule) are what set the behavior up. Use BBS as a supplement to hazard control, never as
a substitute. If a stem describes an unguarded machine and asks for the fix, "observe the
workers' behavior" is wrong.

**Motivation theories to recognize:**

| Theory | Content |
|---|---|
| **Maslow's hierarchy** | Physiological → **safety** → belonging → esteem → self-actualization. Lower needs dominate until met |
| **Herzberg's two-factor** | **Hygiene factors** (pay, conditions, policy, supervision) prevent dissatisfaction but don't motivate; **motivators** (achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth) drive satisfaction |
| **McGregor Theory X / Y** | X: people avoid work, need control. Y: people are self-directed and seek responsibility. Your theory shapes your safety program design |
| **Vroom's expectancy** | Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence. Any factor at zero zeroes the motivation |
| **Reinforcement (Skinner)** | Positive reinforcement, negative reinforcement, punishment, extinction. **Positive reinforcement is the most effective for building behavior** |

## 8. Human and organizational performance

**Human error types (Reason):**

| Type | Description | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Slip** | Right intention, wrong execution — attention failure | Design: forcing functions, differentiation, checklists |
| **Lapse** | Memory failure — forgot a step | Job aids, checklists, reminders |
| **Mistake** | Wrong intention — the plan itself was wrong (rule-based or knowledge-based) | Training, procedures, decision support |
| **Violation** | Deliberate deviation — routine, situational, or exceptional | Understand *why*: usually the procedure is unworkable or the environment rewards the shortcut |

**HOP / Human and Organizational Performance principles:** people make mistakes · error-likely
situations are predictable and manageable · individual behavior is influenced by
organizational processes and values · people achieve high performance largely because of the
encouragement and reinforcement they receive from leaders and peers · **events can be
avoided by understanding why they happen and applying the lessons.**

The HOP reframe: **"human error is not a cause; it's a symptom of trouble deeper in the
system."** Blame ends the investigation exactly where the learning starts. Expect a question
that offers "the employee failed to follow the procedure" as a root cause — it isn't one.

**Performance-shaping factors:** time pressure, fatigue, workload (both **overload and
underload** degrade performance), distraction, poor procedures, inadequate tools, complexity,
unfamiliarity, stress, poor human-machine interface, environmental conditions, and
communication failures.

**Situation awareness — Endsley's three levels:**

1. **Perception** of the elements in the environment
2. **Comprehension** of what they mean
3. **Projection** of their future state

Most SA failures happen at **Level 1 — the person never perceived the cue.** Attention
tunneling, distraction, workload, and poor display design are the causes.

**Fatigue:** performance degradation after ~17 hours awake is comparable to a 0.05% BAC; at
~24 hours, roughly 0.10%. Circadian lows fall around 3–5 a.m. and again mid-afternoon.
Fatigue risk management: shift design (forward-rotating, limited consecutive nights),
overtime limits, nap policies, commute risk, and education.

**Risk perception biases:** optimism bias ("it won't happen to me"), **normalization of
deviance** (repeated success without consequence makes the deviation the new normal — the
Challenger mechanism), availability heuristic (recent/vivid events feel more likely),
anchoring, confirmation bias, groupthink, and the dread/familiarity dimensions (people fear
unfamiliar, catastrophic, involuntary risks disproportionately to their statistics).

**Workload management:** prioritization, task shedding, cross-checking, and staffing. Both
overload and underload (vigilance decrement, automation complacency) degrade performance.

## 9. Communication

**Basic model:** sender → encode → **message/channel** → decode → receiver → **feedback**.
Noise degrades every step. **Feedback is what makes it communication rather than
broadcasting** — and the reason a sign-in sheet isn't training.

**Barriers:** language and literacy, jargon and acronyms, cultural differences, physical
noise, hierarchy and fear, information overload, distraction, assumptions about shared
context, and channel mismatch.

**Channel selection:** rich channels (face-to-face, then video, then phone) for ambiguous,
emotional, or high-stakes messages; lean channels (email, memo, posting) for simple,
unambiguous, documentable ones. Delivering bad news or discussing an incident by email is a
channel mismatch.

**Active listening:** attend, don't interrupt, paraphrase to confirm understanding, ask
open questions, observe nonverbals, and suspend the rebuttal you're composing.

**Risk communication (Covello's principles):** accept and involve the public/workforce as a
partner · plan carefully and evaluate · listen to the audience's specific concerns ·
be honest, frank, and open · coordinate with other credible sources · meet the needs of the
media · **speak clearly and with compassion**. Under high stress, people absorb far less —
so lead with the three key messages, keep them short, and repeat them.

**Persuasion and influence for safety professionals** (who typically have responsibility
without authority): speak in the language of the audience — production speaks in downtime
and throughput, finance in cost and risk exposure, executives in liability and reputation.
Bring data, propose a solution rather than a problem, build coalitions before the meeting,
and pilot small to prove the case.

**Written communication:** know the audience, lead with the conclusion, keep it short, make
the ask explicit, and separate fact from recommendation. In an investigation report, keep
opinion and legal conclusions out of it — it's discoverable (Domain 9).
