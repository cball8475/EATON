# Domain 5 — Emergency Preparedness and Response (10%)

~18 scored items. Definition- and structure-heavy: know what goes in a plan, who's in
charge, and the difference between preparedness, response, recovery, and mitigation.

---

## 1. The four phases of emergency management

| Phase | When | Activities |
|---|---|---|
| **Mitigation / prevention** | Before | Eliminate or reduce the hazard: siting, structural hardening, redundant utilities, process changes, land use |
| **Preparedness** | Before | Plans, training, drills, equipment, mutual aid, supplies, communications |
| **Response** | During | Life safety, incident stabilization, property/environmental protection — **in that order of priority** |
| **Recovery** | After | Restoration of operations, business continuity, repair, employee support, lessons learned |

**Response priorities, always in this order: 1) life safety, 2) incident stabilization,
3) property and environmental protection.** Any answer that puts equipment ahead of people
is wrong.

## 2. OSHA's plan requirements

### Emergency Action Plan — 29 CFR 1910.38

**Must be in writing** and kept in the workplace, **unless the employer has 10 or fewer
employees**, in which case it may be communicated orally.

Required minimum elements:

1. Procedures for **reporting** a fire or other emergency
2. Procedures for **emergency evacuation**, including type of evacuation and exit route
   assignments
3. Procedures for employees who **remain to operate critical plant operations** before they
   evacuate
4. Procedures to **account for all employees** after evacuation
5. Procedures for employees performing **rescue or medical duties**
6. **Name or job title** of every employee who may be contacted for more information

Also required: an **employee alarm system** (1910.165) distinctive for each purpose, and
training — a designated group of employees trained to assist in safe and orderly
evacuation, with review of the plan at initial assignment, when responsibilities change, and
when the plan changes.

### Fire Prevention Plan — 29 CFR 1910.39

Same 10-employee written/oral rule. Elements:

1. List of **major fire hazards**, ignition sources, handling and storage procedures, and
   the control measures for each
2. **Housekeeping** procedures for accumulation of flammable and combustible waste
3. **Maintenance** procedures for equipment installed to prevent or control ignition sources
4. **Name or job title** of employees responsible for maintaining fire-control equipment
   and those responsible for controlling fuel source hazards

### Other plan mandates

| Plan | Trigger |
|---|---|
| **Emergency Response Plan** — 1910.120(q) | Employers whose employees **respond** to hazardous substance releases |
| **Emergency Action Plan** — 1910.120(q)(1) exemption | If employees are to **evacuate** rather than respond, an EAP under 1910.38 suffices |
| **Confined space rescue** — 1910.146(k) | Rescue service evaluation, practice at least annually |
| **PSM emergency planning** — 1910.119(n) | Covered processes |
| **EPA RMP** — 40 CFR 68 | Threshold quantities of listed regulated substances |
| **SPCC** — 40 CFR 112 | Oil storage; see Domain 7 |
| **Bloodborne pathogens exposure control plan** — 1910.1030 | Occupational exposure to blood/OPIM |

**NFPA 1600** — *Standard on Continuity, Emergency, and Crisis Management* — is the consensus
standard for a full program and the basis for the private-sector preparedness (PS-Prep)
program. **ISO 22301** is the international business continuity management standard.

## 3. Sources of emergencies

### Natural

Hurricane/tropical cyclone · tornado · flood · earthquake · winter storm · wildfire ·
extreme heat · lightning · tsunami · landslide.

Planning distinctions the exam tests:

- **Tornado → shelter in place**, in an interior room on the lowest floor, away from
  windows, under a sturdy structure. **Never evacuate into the open.** Reinforced interior
  stairwells and restrooms are typical shelters.
- **Hurricane → evacuate**, with lead time. The whole point is that hurricanes are
  forecastable days out and tornadoes are minutes out.
- **Earthquake → drop, cover, hold on**; do not run outside during shaking (falling façade
  material is the hazard); evacuate after shaking stops. Non-structural bracing (racking,
  gas lines, transformers) is the mitigation.
- **Flood → know the flood zone**, elevate critical equipment, never drive through moving
  water. FEMA flood maps define the 100-year (1% annual chance) floodplain.
- **Lightning → 30/30 rule**: seek shelter when the flash-to-bang is 30 seconds or less;
  wait **30 minutes** after the last thunder. No safe outdoor location.

### Human-caused

Fire and explosion · hazardous material release · structural collapse · transportation
incident · utility failure · cyberattack · civil disturbance · sabotage · terrorism ·
**workplace violence** · active shooter.

### Biological

Pandemic and epidemic disease · bioterrorism · foodborne outbreak · legionella · mold.

**Pandemic planning** elements: continuity of operations with high absenteeism (plan for
30–40%), cross-training and succession depth, remote work, engineering controls (ventilation
and filtration — outdoor air, MERV-13+, HEPA, upper-room UVGI), administrative controls
(distancing, staggered shifts, screening), PPE and respiratory protection, sick-leave
policies that don't punish staying home, communication, and supply chain redundancy.

## 4. Preparedness elements

### Evacuation

- **Primary and secondary routes**, posted maps with a "you are here" orientation
- **Assembly areas** upwind and a safe distance away, with an alternate; away from the
  building collapse zone and out of the way of responding apparatus
- **Accountability** — head count by supervisor or department, with a way to report
  missing persons to the IC. This is required by 1910.38 and it's the step most often
  missing
- **Assistance for people with disabilities** — designated buddies, areas of refuge, and
  evacuation chairs. Do **not** use elevators unless they're a fire-service-rated evacuation
  elevator
- **Shelter-in-place** for external hazards (chemical release, tornado, active threat):
  interior room, shut down HVAC and seal openings for a chemical release, communicate,
  monitor
- **Partial vs. full evacuation** — relocation, horizontal evacuation, or defend-in-place
  are appropriate for hospitals and high-rises

### Drills and exercises (the HSEEP progression)

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Seminar / workshop** | Orientation to the plan; no simulation |
| **Tabletop** | Discussion-based, facilitated walk-through of a scenario. Cheap, catches plan gaps |
| **Drill** | Single function, single agency — an evacuation drill, an extinguisher drill |
| **Functional exercise** | Multiple functions, command and control tested, simulated field response |
| **Full-scale** | Multiple agencies, actual mobilization and deployment of resources |

Build in that order. A full-scale exercise on an untested plan wastes the exercise.

**After-action review (AAR)** and an **improvement plan** with assigned owners and dates
close the loop. An exercise without a documented improvement plan doesn't count.

**Frequency:** evacuation drills at least annually (more often for high-hazard, health care,
education, and where required by local code); confined-space rescue practice at least
annually; fire brigade training quarterly/annually per 1910.156.

### Worksite security

Layered: perimeter (fencing, lighting, CPTED principles — natural surveillance, access
control, territorial reinforcement) → access control (badging, visitor management, escort
policy) → interior (locked areas, cameras, alarms) → asset-specific. Coordinate with local
law enforcement; know your response times, especially at a site outside city limits.

### Medical and first aid

- **1910.151** — a person or persons **adequately trained to render first aid** must be
  available **in the absence of an infirmary, clinic, or hospital in near proximity**. OSHA
  interprets "near proximity" as roughly **3–4 minutes** response for life-threatening
  injuries and 15 minutes otherwise.
- **AEDs** — not required by OSHA, strongly recommended; survival drops ~7–10% per minute
  without defibrillation. Target a 3-minute retrieval-to-shock.
- **Emergency eyewash and shower — ANSI/ISEA Z358.1**: within **10 seconds / 55 ft** of
  the hazard and on the same level with an unobstructed path (**immediately adjacent** for
  strong acids/caustics); **15-minute** continuous flush; **tepid water 60–100 °F**;
  eyewash flow **0.4 gpm**, shower **20 gpm**; hands-free once activated; **activate weekly**
  (plumbed) and inspect annually. Gravity-fed units need weekly checks too.
- Bloodborne pathogens exposure control for responders — see Domain 6.

### Communications

- **Primary and backup** systems (PA, radio, mass notification, text/app, runners)
- Alarm signals distinctive by purpose (1910.165); train employees on what each means
- **Crisis communication**: one designated spokesperson, pre-drafted holding statements,
  a defined approval chain, and coordination with legal. Tell the truth, tell it fast, and
  say what you're doing about it
- Employee family notification procedures, and support for affected employees (EAP, CISM)

### Mutual aid and external coordination

Written mutual aid agreements with neighboring facilities and local responders; a **pre-
incident plan** given to the fire department (site plan, hazardous materials, shutoffs,
hydrants, confined spaces, access); LEPC coordination under EPCRA; annual walk-throughs.

## 5. Response — the Incident Command System

**NIMS** (National Incident Management System) is the overall framework; **ICS** is the
on-scene command structure inside it. ICS came out of the 1970s California wildfires and is
required for anyone receiving federal preparedness funding.

**Five major functional areas:**

| Function | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| **Command** | Overall authority; sets objectives, strategy, priorities. The IC is the only position always filled |
| **Operations** | Tactical work — does the actual response |
| **Planning** | Collects/evaluates information, prepares the Incident Action Plan, tracks resources |
| **Logistics** | Provides resources, facilities, services, and support |
| **Finance/Administration** | Cost tracking, procurement, compensation and claims, time |

**Command staff** (report directly to the IC): **Safety Officer** (the only position with the
authority to **stop unsafe operations immediately**), **Public Information Officer**,
**Liaison Officer**.

**Core ICS principles:**

- **Unity of command** — every person reports to exactly one supervisor
- **Unified command** — multiple agencies with jurisdiction share command and set shared
  objectives; they do **not** each run separate operations. Unity of command and unified
  command are different concepts, and the exam contrasts them
- **Span of control** — 3 to 7 subordinates, **5 optimal**
- **Modular, scalable organization** — activate only the positions the incident needs; the
  IC holds every function not delegated
- **Common terminology** — plain language, no radio codes
- **Incident Action Plan** per operational period
- **Management by objectives**, comprehensive resource management, integrated
  communications, accountability, and a designated **Incident Command Post**

**Transfer of command** happens with a face-to-face briefing and is announced to everyone.
The first arriving qualified person is the IC by default.

### HAZWOPER — 29 CFR 1910.120

**Five levels of emergency response training:**

| Level | Training | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **First responder awareness** | Sufficient training/experience to demonstrate competency | Recognize a release, **notify**, secure the area. **Takes no action to stop it** |
| **First responder operations** | ≥ 8 hours | **Defensive** actions — diking, damming, absorbing from a distance. Does **not** try to stop the release at the point of leak |
| **Hazardous materials technician** | ≥ 24 hours + operations competencies | **Offensive** — approaches the point of release to **plug, patch, or stop** it |
| **Hazardous materials specialist** | ≥ 24 hours + technician competencies | Technician support with specific knowledge; liaison with government authorities |
| **On-scene incident commander** | ≥ 24 hours + operations competencies | Assumes control of the incident beyond the awareness level |

**The operations/technician line is the classic question: defensive vs. offensive.**

**Site worker training** (cleanup operations at uncontrolled hazardous waste sites):
**40 hours** off-site plus **3 days** supervised field experience; **8 hours annual
refresher**. Supervisors add 8 hours. Occasional site workers and workers at sites with
characterized low exposure: 24 hours plus 1 day.

**EPA PPE levels:**

| Level | Respiratory | Skin | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | **SCBA (pressure-demand)** | **Fully encapsulating, vapor-tight** suit | Highest respiratory **and** skin/vapor protection needed; unknown atmosphere |
| **B** | SCBA or supplied air | Splash protection, **non-vapor-tight** (hooded chemical-resistant suit) | Highest **respiratory** protection needed, lesser skin protection — the usual choice for an unknown that's known to be non-skin-hazardous |
| **C** | **Air-purifying respirator** | Chemical-resistant clothing | The contaminant and concentration are **known**, criteria for APR use are met, and the atmosphere has adequate oxygen |
| **D** | None | Work uniform | Nuisance only; **no respiratory or skin hazard** |

**Level B is the initial choice for entry into an unknown** where skin absorption isn't
suspected — that's counterintuitive and therefore tested.

**Work zones:** **Exclusion (hot) zone** — contamination present, highest PPE.
**Contamination reduction (warm) zone** — contains the decontamination corridor.
**Support (cold) zone** — clean, command post, staging. Entry and exit through **access
control points** only, and decon happens in the warm zone.

**Medical surveillance** required for HAZWOPER workers exposed above PELs 30+ days/year,
those wearing respirators 30+ days/year, HAZMAT team members, and anyone showing exposure
signs. Baseline, annual, and termination exams.

## 6. Recovery and business continuity

**Business Impact Analysis (BIA)** identifies critical functions, the impact of their loss
over time, dependencies, and the resources needed to restore them. It's the input to the
continuity plan — **you do the BIA first**.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **MTD / MAO** | Maximum tolerable downtime / maximum acceptable outage before unacceptable harm |
| **RTO** — Recovery Time Objective | How fast a function must be back up. Must be ≤ MTD |
| **RPO** — Recovery Point Objective | How much **data loss** is acceptable, measured backward in time from the incident |
| **Critical function** | One whose loss causes unacceptable harm inside the MTD |

**Continuity strategies:** alternate sites (**hot** = fully equipped and running, **warm** =
equipped but needs data/staff, **cold** = space only), work-from-home, reciprocal agreements,
cloud replication, cross-training, vendor redundancy and dual sourcing, and pre-negotiated
contracts for restoration services.

**Succession and delegation of authority** must be documented — who takes command if the
plant manager can't be reached. Emergency plans that name one person by name and no
alternate fail their first real test.

**Recovery sequence:** damage assessment → safety/structural clearance → salvage and
restoration → resumption of critical functions → full restoration → **AAR and plan revision**.

**Insurance interfaces:** property, business interruption (covers lost income during
restoration), extra expense, contingent business interruption (a supplier's loss), builder's
risk. Document everything for the claim.

## 7. Workplace violence prevention

**The four types (the standard typology):**

| Type | Perpetrator | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Type I — Criminal intent** | No legitimate relationship to the business | Robbery of a convenience store. **The most fatal type overall** |
| **Type II — Customer/client** | Receives services from the organization | Patient assaults a nurse. **The most common type of non-fatal workplace assault**, concentrated in health care and social services |
| **Type III — Worker-on-worker** | Current or former employee | Coworker or supervisor violence |
| **Type IV — Personal relationship** | Has a relationship with an employee, not the workplace | Domestic violence following a worker to the job |

**Program elements:** management commitment and employee participation · **worksite
hazard analysis** (records review, walkthrough, employee survey) · hazard prevention and
control (engineering — barriers, alarms, lighting, CPTED, controlled access; administrative —
staffing levels, cash handling, escorts, no-lone-work policies) · **safety and health
training** · **recordkeeping and program evaluation**. That's the OSHA guidelines framework
and it maps onto the general program elements — a repeated exam pattern.

**No OSHA standard for workplace violence** in general industry; cited under the **General
Duty Clause**, with published guidelines for health care/social services, late-night retail,
and taxi drivers. Several states have their own health-care-specific requirements. (Watch
for a federal health-care standard in rulemaking — verify current status.)

**Threat assessment teams** — multidisciplinary (HR, security, legal, EHS, EAP, sometimes
law enforcement), assess concerning behavior rather than profile individuals, and manage the
case over time. **Behavioral warning signs** are indicators for intervention, not a
predictive profile; profiling doesn't work and the exam won't reward it.

**Active shooter response: RUN – HIDE – FIGHT**, in that order of preference. Run if there's
an accessible escape path, leave belongings, keep hands visible for law enforcement. Hide out
of view, lock and barricade, silence phones. Fight only as a last resort, aggressively and
with improvised weapons. Law enforcement's first priority is stopping the shooter, **not**
treating the wounded — that's a widely tested detail.

**Domestic violence** carries into the workplace: workplace safety planning, protective order
support, parking/escort accommodations, and reception awareness are the program pieces.

## 8. Lone workers

A lone worker is anyone working out of sight or earshot of another person, including remote
sites, night shifts, service technicians, and off-hours maintenance.

**Program elements:**

1. **Risk assessment** — a task acceptable with two people may be prohibited alone.
   High-risk work that should **never** be done alone: confined space entry, energized
   electrical work, work at height with fall arrest (rescue requirement), hot work,
   HAZMAT handling, and heavy machinery operation
2. **Check-in / monitoring** — scheduled check-ins with a defined escalation if a check-in
   is missed, GPS and man-down devices, automated alerts, and lone-worker apps
3. **Communication** — reliable coverage verified for the location; satellite where cellular
   fails
4. **Training** — hazard-specific plus emergency self-rescue and reporting
5. **Emergency response** — a defined plan for someone who cannot call for help. Response
   time matters more than usual because there's no bystander
6. **Violence and security** for field workers and late-night retail

**The key regulatory hooks:** confined space **requires an attendant** (so entry is never a
lone-worker task); fall arrest requires **prompt rescue**; and 1910.269 requires a second
person for certain energized line work. Beyond those, it's General Duty Clause territory.
