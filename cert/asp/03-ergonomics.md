# Domain 3 — Ergonomics (8%)

~14 scored items. Small domain, high yield — the content is finite and the questions are
predictable. Master the risk factors, the assessment tools, and the NIOSH equation and
you'll take most of these.

---

## 1. What ergonomics is

**Fitting the job to the worker, not the worker to the job.** Every conceptual question in
this domain resolves back to that sentence. If an answer choice puts the burden on the
person (training them to lift correctly, telling them to stretch) and another modifies the
workstation, the workstation answer wins.

**Physical ergonomics** — anthropometry, biomechanics, posture, materials handling.
**Cognitive ergonomics** — mental workload, decision making, human-computer interaction,
attention.
**Organizational/macroergonomics** — work design, shift schedules, teamwork, staffing.

OSHA has **no ergonomics standard.** The 2000 standard was repealed by the Congressional
Review Act in 2001. Ergonomic hazards are cited under the **General Duty Clause 5(a)(1)**,
and OSHA publishes industry-specific voluntary guidelines (nursing homes, poultry, retail
grocery, shipyards). That fact — no standard, GDC enforcement — is a near-certain question.

## 2. Elements of an ergonomics program

1. **Management commitment and worker involvement** — workers know where the discomfort is
2. **Training** — recognition of risk factors and early symptoms, for workers and supervisors
3. **Hazard identification and risk assessment** — proactive (job analysis, screening tools)
   and reactive (injury data, workers' comp, discomfort surveys)
4. **Hazard prevention and control** — engineering first, then work practice, then
   administrative, then PPE (which barely exists in ergonomics — anti-vibration gloves and
   knee pads about covers it)
5. **Early symptom reporting and medical management** — the single highest-leverage element.
   Early intervention converts a would-be surgery into a job modification
6. **Program evaluation** — trend the leading and lagging measures

**Sources of data for identifying problem jobs:** OSHA 300 logs, workers' comp claims, first
aid records, absenteeism, turnover by department, discomfort/symptom surveys, worker
suggestions, quality and productivity defects (ergonomic problems often show up as scrap
before they show up as injuries).

## 3. Risk factors

| Factor | What raises risk |
|---|---|
| **Force** | High exertion; pinch grip vs. power grip (a pinch grip requires ~5× the muscle force for the same held weight); gloves that reduce grip; slippery surfaces |
| **Repetition** | Short cycle times, high frequency, insufficient recovery. A cycle time <30 s, or the same motions >50% of the cycle, is a common screening trigger |
| **Awkward posture** | Deviation from neutral — reaching above the shoulder, below the knee, behind the torso; wrist flexion/extension/ulnar deviation; neck flexion >20°, trunk flexion >20°, twisting; squatting/kneeling |
| **Static posture** | Sustained holding without movement. Blood flow to the muscle is restricted; fatigue accrues even at low force |
| **Contact stress** | Hard/sharp edges pressing on tissue — palm against a tool handle, forearm on a bench edge |
| **Vibration** | Hand-arm (HAV, from powered hand tools) and whole-body (WBV, from vehicles and platforms) |
| **Environment** | Cold (reduces dexterity and grip sensation, raises applied force), poor lighting (drives head/neck posture), heat, glare |
| **Work organization** | Machine-paced work, incentive pay, overtime, no job rotation, no control over pace |

**Risk multiplies when factors combine.** A forceful exertion is one thing; a forceful
exertion in an awkward posture, repeated, in the cold, is a different order of risk. Exam
stems load multiple factors on purpose.

## 4. Work-related musculoskeletal disorders

**MSD** = injury or disorder of muscles, nerves, tendons, joints, cartilage, or spinal
discs. **WMSD** adds that the work environment and performance of work contributed
significantly. Note the wording: work need not be the *sole* cause.

| Disorder | Location / mechanism | Signature |
|---|---|---|
| **Carpal tunnel syndrome** | Median nerve compression at the wrist | Numbness/tingling in thumb, index, middle, and half the ring finger; night pain; thenar atrophy. **Phalen's** and **Tinel's** tests |
| **Tendinitis / tenosynovitis** | Inflammation of tendon / tendon sheath | Repetitive motion, localized pain and swelling |
| **De Quervain's** | Tenosynovitis of the thumb tendons | Wringing/forceful thumb use. **Finkelstein's test** |
| **Trigger finger** | Stenosing tenosynovitis of a flexor tendon | Finger locks in flexion; tool triggers, sustained gripping |
| **Lateral epicondylitis ("tennis elbow")** | Extensor tendon origin, outside of the elbow | Forceful wrist extension, supination |
| **Medial epicondylitis ("golfer's elbow")** | Flexor tendon origin, inside of the elbow | Forceful wrist flexion, pronation |
| **Rotator cuff tendinitis / impingement** | Shoulder | Overhead work — the single worst shoulder posture |
| **Thoracic outlet syndrome** | Neurovascular compression at the neck/shoulder | Overhead work, carrying heavy loads, backpack straps |
| **HAVS / vibration white finger (Raynaud's)** | Vascular and neural damage from HAV | Blanching of fingers, cold sensitivity, loss of sensation and grip |
| **Low back disorders** | Discs, muscles, ligaments | Lifting, bending, twisting, WBV, prolonged sitting |
| **Hand-arm nerve entrapments** | Ulnar (Guyon's canal / cubital tunnel) | Ring and little finger symptoms — **not** carpal tunnel |

**The pattern to memorize: thumb/index/middle = median = carpal tunnel; ring/little = ulnar.**
That distinction is asked directly.

**Recordability:** an MSD is recorded like any other case — under the general criteria. There
is no separate MSD column since 2011 (the proposed column was withdrawn). Signs and symptoms
alone are not recordable without one of the recording criteria (days away, restricted work,
medical treatment beyond first aid, etc.).

## 5. Assessment tools — what each one does

| Tool | Assesses | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **NIOSH Lifting Equation** | Two-handed manual lifting | RWL and Lifting Index |
| **RULA** (Rapid Upper Limb Assessment) | **Upper body** — neck, trunk, upper limbs | Grand score 1–7 → action levels 1–4 |
| **REBA** (Rapid Entire Body Assessment) | **Whole body**, including legs; handles unpredictable and dynamic postures | Score 1–15 → risk levels |
| **Strain Index (RSI)** | Distal upper extremity (hand/wrist) disorders | Multiplicative score; >7 = hazardous |
| **ACGIH TLV for HAL** | Hand activity level vs. peak force | Plot against action limit and TLV lines |
| **Snook / Liberty Mutual tables** | Manual **push, pull, carry**, and lift | % of the population capable of the task |
| **Washington State Ergonomic Rule checklists** | Caution/hazard zone screening | Pass/fail screening |
| **ACGIH TLV for lifting** | Lifting, by hand location and frequency/duration | Weight limit tables |
| **OWAS** | Whole-body postures, sampling-based | Action categories 1–4 |
| **QEC** (Quick Exposure Check) | Whole body, includes the worker's own assessment | Exposure scores |
| **Anthropometry** | Body dimensions for design | Percentile-based design specs |

**Selection is what's tested.** A cashier's wrist and shoulder → **RULA**. A nurse
repositioning a patient (whole body, unpredictable) → **REBA**. Cartons off a pallet →
**NIOSH**. Rolling a cart across a plant → **Snook tables** (NIOSH does not cover push/pull
or carrying, and never one-handed lifting).

**RULA action levels:** 1–2 acceptable · 3–4 investigate further · 5–6 investigate and
change soon · 7 investigate and change **immediately**.

**REBA risk levels:** 1 negligible · 2–3 low · 4–7 medium · 8–10 high · 11–15 very high.

**NIOSH equation** — full detail in [`01-math-and-physics.md`](01-math-and-physics.md).
The assumptions that void the equation: one-handed lifting, lifting for over 8 hours,
lifting while seated or kneeling, lifting in a restricted workspace, unstable objects,
carrying/pushing/pulling, wheelbarrows/shoveling, high-speed lifting (>30 in/s), poor
foot/floor coupling, and unfavorable environments (outside 66–79 °F, above 95% RH).

## 6. Anthropometry and workstation design

**Three design strategies, and knowing when to use each is the question:**

| Strategy | Use when | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Design for extremes** | One tail of the distribution constrains the design | Clearance (doorway height, legroom) → design for the **large** user (95th percentile male). Reach (control placement, shelf height) → design for the **small** user (5th percentile female) |
| **Design for adjustability** | Best solution when feasible; accommodate 5th–95th percentile | Chair height, monitor arm, adjustable-height workbench |
| **Design for the average** | Only when adjustability is impractical and extremes don't matter | Door handle height, checkout counter |

**"Design for the average" is almost always the wrong answer** — there is no average person
across multiple dimensions. Adjustability first, then extremes.

**Clearance vs. reach is the trap:** clearance dimensions use the **large** user, reach
dimensions use the **small** user. Get them backwards and you've designed a workstation that
fits nobody.

### Neutral postures and design targets

- **Elbow height** is the reference for work-surface height. Precision work: **above** elbow
  height (with forearm support). Light assembly: at elbow height. Heavy/force work:
  **below** elbow height, so the worker uses body weight.
- Work within the **primary reach zone** (forearm sweep, elbow at side) for frequent items;
  the secondary zone (full arm extension) for occasional items. Nothing frequent behind the
  shoulder plane.
- Shoulders relaxed, upper arms at the sides, wrists straight, no ulnar deviation.
- **Sit/stand**: alternate. Prolonged standing → anti-fatigue matting, footrest to shift
  weight, sit/stand stool. Prolonged sitting → lumbar support, feet flat or footrest, thighs
  parallel to the floor, no pressure behind the knees.
- **Monitor**: top of the screen at or slightly below eye height, ~20–40 in away (roughly
  arm's length), tilted slightly back. Bifocal wearers need the monitor **lower** — they
  tilt their head back to read through the bottom lens otherwise.
- **Keyboard/mouse** at elbow height, wrists neutral and floating (a wrist rest is for
  resting between typing, not while typing), mouse adjacent to the keyboard on the same
  plane.
- **Lighting**: 30–50 foot-candles for general office; more for detailed inspection tasks.
  Control **glare** — indirect lighting, task lighting, matte surfaces, position monitors
  perpendicular to windows.
- **Overhead work** is the highest-risk posture in a plant. Elevated platforms, articulating
  tool arms, and repositioning the work are the fixes; job rotation is a stopgap.

## 7. Manual material handling

**Control hierarchy for MMH:**

1. **Eliminate the manual handling** — conveyors, chutes, gravity feed, redesign the process
   so the material never needs lifting
2. **Mechanical assist** — hoists, vacuum lifters, manipulators, lift tables, scissor lifts,
   pallet positioners/turntables, cart with proper casters
3. **Reduce the load** — smaller package quantities, break down unit loads, lighter
   containers, better handles
4. **Improve the layout** — raise the work to knuckle height (~30 in), eliminate the
   horizontal reach, eliminate twisting by repositioning, provide clearance for the feet
5. **Work practice** — two-person lifts, team lifting protocols, job rotation
6. **Training** — necessary, but by itself the weakest control

**"Safe lifting training" alone does not reduce injury rates.** The evidence is consistent
on this and the exam reflects it. If training is one answer choice and a lift table is
another, choose the lift table.

**Back belts:** NIOSH concluded there is **insufficient evidence** that back belts prevent
injury in uninjured workers, and cautions they may create a false sense of security. They
are not PPE under OSHA. Know the NIOSH position.

**Lifting technique fundamentals** (still worth knowing as a question stem): keep the load
close, no twisting (turn with the feet), lift with the legs, avoid lifting above the shoulder
or below the knee, get a firm grip, plan the route, and test the load first.

**Pushing beats pulling** — better posture, better use of body weight, better visibility, and
lower spinal compression. Snook/Liberty Mutual tables give the acceptable initial and
sustained forces by population percentile. Casters matter more than people expect:
large-diameter, well-maintained, correctly configured casters can cut required force in half.

## 8. Work practice and administrative controls

- **Job rotation** — rotate between jobs with *different* risk factors. Rotating a worker
  from one repetitive wrist task to another repetitive wrist task accomplishes nothing. That
  distinction gets tested.
- **Work-rest cycles and micro-breaks** — brief, frequent breaks beat long, infrequent ones
  for static and repetitive work.
- **Job enlargement / enrichment** — increasing task variety reduces repetition and improves
  engagement.
- **Early symptom intervention** — encourage reporting, evaluate promptly, modify the job.
  Requires a culture where reporting isn't punished (ties to Domain 8).
- **Exoskeletons** — passive (spring/counterbalance) and active (powered). Real load transfer
  for overhead and back-support tasks, but they shift load rather than remove it, may create
  new stresses, and need careful fitting and a trial. They are a **work-practice/PPE-adjacent
  control, not an engineering control** — the hazard is unchanged.
- **Stretching programs** — popular, weak evidence for injury prevention. Don't pick them
  over an engineering fix.

## 9. Vibration

**Hand-arm vibration (HAV)** — powered hand tools (grinders, chippers, impact wrenches,
chainsaws). Effects: HAVS/vibration white finger (vascular), sensorineural damage
(numbness, loss of dexterity), and carpal tunnel. Cold exposure makes it worse.
Controls: low-vibration tool selection (the highest-leverage control), anti-vibration
mounts and handles, tool maintenance and sharp bits, reduced grip force, exposure time
limits, warm hands and gloves, and anti-vibration gloves (limited effectiveness at low
frequencies).

**Whole-body vibration (WBV)** — vehicle seats, forklifts, heavy equipment, vibrating
platforms. Associated with low back pain and disc degeneration. Controls: suspension
seats, tire and pavement maintenance, reduced speed, seat design and adjustment, and
limiting duration.

Measured as frequency-weighted acceleration in m/s²; ACGIH publishes TLVs for both,
expressed as an acceleration limit for a given daily exposure duration. The relevant ISO
standards are **ISO 5349** (HAV) and **ISO 2631** (WBV).
