# Domain 9 — Legal (5%)

~9 scored items. The smallest domain, and the cheapest to master — it's almost entirely
definitions and structures. Do not skip it because it's small; nine items is the difference
between passing and re-testing in six weeks.

---

## 1. The OSH Act of 1970

**Purpose:** "to assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe
and healthful working conditions."

**Three agencies created (know which does what — it's asked directly):**

| Agency | Role | Where it sits |
|---|---|---|
| **OSHA** | **Standards setting and enforcement** | Department of **Labor** |
| **NIOSH** | **Research** and recommendations (RELs, HHEs, criteria documents). **No enforcement authority** | CDC / Department of **Health and Human Services** |
| **OSHRC** | **Independent** review commission that adjudicates contested citations | Independent federal agency |

### Employer duties — Section 5(a)

- **5(a)(1) — the General Duty Clause:** furnish each employee a place of employment "free
  from **recognized hazards** that are causing or are likely to cause **death or serious
  physical harm**."
- **5(a)(2):** comply with occupational safety and health standards promulgated under the Act.

**Four elements OSHA must prove for a General Duty Clause citation:**

1. A **hazard** existed
2. The hazard was **recognized** — by the employer, the industry, or common sense
3. The hazard was causing or likely to cause **death or serious physical harm**
4. A **feasible and useful method** existed to correct the hazard

The GDC only applies where **no specific standard covers the hazard**. It's how OSHA reaches
ergonomics, workplace violence, heat, and combustible dust in general industry.

**Employee duties — Section 5(b):** employees shall comply with standards and rules
applicable to their own actions. **OSHA does not cite employees** — enforcement runs against
the employer.

### Employee rights

File a complaint and request an inspection · have a representative accompany the inspector ·
talk privately with the inspector · access their **exposure and medical records**
(1910.1020) · access injury and illness records (300 logs) · receive training in a language
they understand · receive copies of test results · **be free from retaliation**.

**Section 11(c) whistleblower protection:** complaints must be filed with OSHA within
**30 days** of the retaliatory action. (Other statutes OSHA administers have longer windows —
e.g., Sarbanes-Oxley 180 days, STAA 180 days. The **OSH Act's own is 30 days**.)

### Standards and how they're structured

- **Vertical (industry-specific)** standards — e.g., 1926 Construction, 1915/1917/1918
  Maritime, 1928 Agriculture. **Horizontal (general)** standards apply across industries.
- **Specific standard beats general standard** where both could apply.
- **Standards hierarchy of citation:** the specific standard first; the general standard
  next; then the General Duty Clause when no standard applies.
- **Incorporation by reference** — OSHA adopts consensus standards (ANSI, NFPA, ASME) into
  the CFR, which makes those editions legally enforceable. Note the incorporated edition is
  usually **older** than the current published edition; the incorporated one governs.
- **Rulemaking:** Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking → **NPRM** in the Federal Register →
  public comment and hearings → final rule → effective date. Challenges go to the Courts of
  Appeals.
- **Variances:** **temporary** (need time to comply, must show a plan and interim employee
  protection, up to 1 year plus renewals), **permanent** (an alternative that is **at least
  as effective** as the standard), experimental, national defense, and interim.

### Inspections

**Priority order (memorize the order):**

1. **Imminent danger**
2. **Fatalities and catastrophes** (severe injury reports)
3. **Complaints and referrals**
4. **Programmed inspections** (targeted by industry hazard rates and emphasis programs)

**Process:** presentation of credentials → **opening conference** → walkaround (with an
employer representative and an authorized employee representative) → employee interviews →
**closing conference** → citations issued within **6 months** of the violation.

**Warrants:** *Marshall v. Barlow's, Inc.* (1978) established that an employer may **refuse
entry and require an administrative warrant**; OSHA can obtain one on a showing of probable
cause or a neutral administrative inspection plan.

### Citations and penalties

| Classification | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Other-than-serious** | A direct relationship to safety and health, but unlikely to cause death or serious physical harm |
| **Serious** | Substantial probability that **death or serious physical harm** could result, and the employer knew or should have known |
| **Willful** | Intentional disregard for, or plain indifference to, the requirements |
| **Repeat** | A substantially similar violation cited within the **previous 5 years** at any of the employer's establishments |
| **Failure to abate** | The violation continues past the abatement date; assessed **per day** |
| **De minimis** | No direct or immediate relationship to safety and health — **notice only, no penalty** |

**Willful violations resulting in an employee death** can carry criminal penalties —
historically a misdemeanor with up to 6 months' imprisonment. Penalty **dollar amounts are
adjusted for inflation every January**; know the categories and the multiples (willful/repeat
run **10×** serious), not the figures.

**Penalty adjustment factors:** gravity (the primary factor) · employer size · good faith ·
history of previous violations.

**Employer response:** post the citation at or near the location of the violation for
**3 working days or until abated, whichever is longer**; contest in writing within
**15 working days** of receipt (a Notice of Contest), or the citation becomes a **final
order not subject to review**. An **informal conference** with the Area Director may be
requested within that same 15-day window and often reduces classifications and penalties —
**but it does not extend the contest deadline.**

### Reporting and recordkeeping — 29 CFR 1904

| Event | Report to OSHA within |
|---|---|
| **Fatality** | **8 hours** (if it occurs within 30 days of the incident) |
| **In-patient hospitalization**, **amputation**, or **loss of an eye** | **24 hours** (if within 24 hours of the incident) |

**Forms:** **300** (Log of Work-Related Injuries and Illnesses) · **300A** (Annual Summary) ·
**301** (Injury and Illness Incident Report, or an equivalent).

- **Post the 300A from February 1 through April 30**, signed by a **company executive**
- **Retain records for 5 years** following the calendar year they cover, and update the 300
  log during that period
- **Privacy cases** (injuries to intimate body parts, sexual assaults, mental illness, HIV/
  hepatitis/TB, needlesticks and sharps injuries from contaminated sharps) — enter
  "**Privacy Case**" instead of the name and keep a separate confidential list
- **Electronic submission** (1904.41) applies to establishments by size and industry;
  requirements have changed repeatedly — verify the current rule
- **Partially exempt** industries (low-hazard NAICS) and employers with **10 or fewer
  employees** at all times during the previous year are exempt from routine recordkeeping —
  but **never exempt from the fatality/hospitalization reporting requirement**

**State plans:** OSHA-approved state programs must be **at least as effective** as federal
OSHA and may be more stringent. There are **22 state plans covering private sector plus
state and local government** and **7 covering state and local government employees only**.
Federal OSHA does not cover state and local government employees, which is why the
public-only plans exist.

## 2. Multi-employer worksites and contractor management

**OSHA's Multi-Employer Citation Policy (CPL 02-00-124)** — four employer categories, and
more than one can be cited for the same hazard:

| Category | Who | Basis for citation |
|---|---|---|
| **Creating** | Caused the hazardous condition | Cited even if their own employees aren't exposed |
| **Exposing** | Their employees are exposed | Cited unless they can show they didn't know, took steps to protect, and asked the controlling/creating employer to correct |
| **Correcting** | Responsible for correcting the hazard (often the specialty contractor engaged to fix or maintain it) | Failure to exercise reasonable care |
| **Controlling** | **General contractor or host with general supervisory authority** — including the power to correct or require correction | Failure to exercise **reasonable care** to detect and prevent violations. Reasonable care is measured by periodic inspections, an effective system for correction, and enforcement |

**The exposing employer is not automatically the only one cited, and the controlling employer
can be cited with no exposed employees of its own.** That's the point of the policy and it's
the question.

**Host/contractor safety management (also a Domain 2 concept):**

1. **Prequalification** — EMR, TRIR/DART history, OSHA citation history, written programs,
   training records, insurance certificates, references
2. **Contract terms** — safety requirements, insurance and indemnification, right to stop
   work, incident reporting obligations, subcontractor flow-down
3. **Orientation** — site-specific hazards, emergency procedures, permits, reporting
4. **Coordination** — pre-job planning, permit systems, daily coordination for simultaneous
   operations (SIMOPS)
5. **Monitoring** — inspections, observations, audits, incident review
6. **Evaluation** — post-job performance review feeding back into prequalification

**The independent contractor tension:** exercising too much day-to-day control over a
contractor's work can create a **joint employer** or **borrowed servant** relationship,
undermining the exclusive-remedy protection of workers' comp and creating direct liability.
But under the multi-employer policy, a host that exercises *no* oversight can be cited as a
controlling employer. Contract language and documented coordination — rather than direct
supervision of the contractor's employees — is how you thread that.

**Temporary workers:** OSHA's Temporary Worker Initiative treats the **staffing agency and
the host as joint employers**, both responsible. Generally the host handles site-specific
hazard training and control, the agency handles general safety training and injury tracking;
the injury is generally recorded by whoever **supervises the worker day-to-day** (usually the
host).

## 3. Contracts and risk transfer

**Contract lifecycle:** need identification → solicitation (RFI/RFP/RFQ) → negotiation →
award and execution → performance and administration → change management → closeout →
records retention.

**Contract terminology to recognize:**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Indemnification / hold harmless** | One party agrees to cover the other's losses. **Broad form** (covers even the indemnitee's own sole negligence) is void in many states; intermediate and limited forms are more common |
| **Additional insured** | Named on the other party's policy, gaining direct coverage rather than relying on the indemnity promise |
| **Certificate of insurance (COI)** | Evidence of coverage — but it is **not** the policy and confers no rights by itself. Verify limits, dates, and endorsements |
| **Waiver of subrogation** | The insurer gives up its right to recover from the other party |
| **Liquidated damages** | Pre-agreed damages for a defined breach (usually delay) |
| **Force majeure** | Excuses performance for defined extraordinary events |
| **Scope of work** | What is actually being bought — where safety requirements must appear to be enforceable |
| **Flow-down** | Requiring subcontractors to accept the same terms |

**The four risk-treatment options** (also Domain 2): **avoid · reduce · transfer · retain.**

**Risk transfer mechanisms:** insurance · contractual indemnification · outsourcing ·
hold-harmless agreements · surety bonds · captives.

**What transfer does and doesn't do — the single most-tested legal concept:** it moves the
**financial consequence**, not the hazard, not the moral responsibility, and generally not
the regulatory duty. **OSHA obligations cannot be contracted away.**

**Insurance basics:**

- **Workers' compensation** — statutory, **no-fault**, and the **exclusive remedy** for
  work-related injury against the employer. The trade-off: the employee gives up the right
  to sue for pain and suffering; the employer gets predictable, limited liability.
  **Exceptions to exclusivity:** intentional torts by the employer (in some states),
  third-party actions (against equipment manufacturers, property owners, other contractors),
  and dual-capacity claims
- **Experience Modification Rate (EMR)** — `Actual losses / Expected losses`. **1.0 is
  average; below 1.0 means better than the class average** and reduces premium. Driven more
  by **claim frequency** than by severity, and it lags 3 years. Widely used as a
  prequalification screen
- **General liability**, **auto liability**, **umbrella/excess**, **property**, **business
  interruption**, **environmental/pollution legal liability**, **professional liability
  (E&O)** — including for **safety consultants**
- **Self-insured retention / deductible / captive** — retention strategies for large
  organizations

## 4. Tort liability and litigation

**Negligence — the four elements:**

1. **Duty** — a legal obligation owed to the plaintiff (a standard of care)
2. **Breach** — failure to meet that standard
3. **Causation** — both **actual** ("but for") and **proximate** (foreseeability)
4. **Damages** — actual harm

**Defenses:** comparative negligence (damages reduced by the plaintiff's share) ·
contributory negligence (in a few states, any plaintiff fault bars recovery) · assumption of
risk · statute of limitations · **the exclusive remedy of workers' comp**.

**Strict liability** — liability without fault, applied to **abnormally dangerous
activities** (blasting, hazardous waste) and to **defective products**.

**Product liability theories:** **design defect** (the whole line is unreasonably dangerous),
**manufacturing defect** (this unit deviated from the design), and **failure to warn**
(inadequate instructions or warnings). A warning does **not** cure a design that could
feasibly have been made safe — that's the hierarchy of controls stated in tort language, and
it's exactly how the exam frames it.

**Standard of care** for a safety professional: what a reasonably prudent safety professional
with similar training would do under similar circumstances. **Holding a certification raises
the standard you'll be measured against** — a real consequence of the ASP/CSP that the exam
points at.

**Litigation concepts:**

- **Discovery** — depositions, interrogatories, requests for production. **Assume every
  safety document you write will be read aloud in a deposition.** Write facts, keep
  speculation and legal conclusions out, and don't editorialize in an incident report
- **Spoliation** — destruction or alteration of evidence, including routine deletion after a
  duty to preserve attaches. Triggers sanctions and adverse-inference instructions. Issue a
  **litigation hold** when litigation is reasonably anticipated
- **Chain of custody** — documented, unbroken possession of physical evidence and samples;
  a gap destroys admissibility
- **Expert witness** — testifies to opinion within their expertise; must meet the
  **Daubert** standard (testable, peer-reviewed, known error rate, generally accepted,
  reliably applied). A **fact witness** testifies only to what they observed
- **Audit privilege** — some states protect voluntary self-audit findings from discovery in
  limited ways; the protection is narrow, inconsistent, and generally does **not** apply to
  OSHA. Do not treat an audit report as privileged. **Attorney-client privilege and
  work-product doctrine** are the reliable protections, which is why counsel sometimes
  directs sensitive audits
- **Subpoena, deposition, and testimony** — coordinate with counsel; never speculate under
  oath

## 5. Records control

| Record | Retention |
|---|---|
| **OSHA 300, 300A, 301** | **5 years** following the year covered |
| **Employee exposure records** (monitoring, SDSs where relevant, biological monitoring) | **30 years** |
| **Employee medical records** | **Duration of employment + 30 years** |
| **Employees employed less than 1 year** | Medical records need not be retained if given to the employee on termination |
| **Bloodborne pathogens training records** | 3 years |
| **Respirator fit test records** | Until the next fit test |
| **Noise exposure measurements** | 2 years |
| **Audiometric test records** | Duration of the employee's employment |
| **Process safety incident investigation reports** | 5 years |
| **Hazardous waste manifests** | 3 years |
| **SPCC plan** | Duration plus 3 years of inspection records |

**29 CFR 1910.1020** — employees, their designated representatives, and OSHA get access to
exposure and medical records; the employer must respond within **15 working days**, at no
cost for the first copy. **Trade secret** protections exist but cannot withhold information
needed for treatment or exposure assessment.

**Worker privacy:** medical records must be kept **separate from personnel files** and
disclosed only as permitted (ADA requirement). Overlapping regimes:

- **ADA** — medical inquiries limited; records confidential and separate
- **GINA** — genetic information, including family medical history, may not be requested or
  used. A wellness-program questionnaire asking about family history is the classic GINA trap
- **HIPAA** — applies to covered entities (health plans, providers, clearinghouses).
  **Employer injury records are generally *not* HIPAA-covered**, which surprises people;
  the ADA and 1910.1020 govern instead
- **State privacy laws** and biometric/wearable data policies — an emerging area with
  consent requirements

**Chain of custody** for samples (IH, drug testing, evidence): documented collection, sealed
containers, signed transfers, and secure storage. Break it and the result is unusable.

## 6. Scope, competence, and ethics

**Know the boundary of your own competence.** The exam repeatedly offers "handle it yourself"
against "refer it to the appropriate professional," and referral is usually correct for:

| Situation | Refer to |
|---|---|
| Structural design, excavation systems >20 ft, engineered anchorages | **Registered professional engineer** |
| Medical diagnosis, fitness-for-duty determination, work restrictions | **PLHCP / occupational physician** |
| Legal interpretation, contract terms, litigation strategy | **Attorney** |
| Cybersecurity of OT/control systems | **Information security professional** |
| Insurance coverage, policy interpretation | **Risk manager / broker** |
| Complex exposure assessment and sampling strategy | **CIH** |
| Complex ergonomic/biomechanical modeling | **CPE** |
| Environmental permitting and remediation | **Environmental professional / PE** |

**BCSP Code of Ethics** — you agree to it before the exam starts. Core obligations: hold
paramount the **safety and health of people**; practice **only within your area of
competence**; act with **honesty, integrity, and impartiality**; issue **objective** public
statements and disclose any interest that could affect judgment; avoid conflicts of interest
and disclose those that can't be avoided; keep confidences except where disclosure is
necessary to protect health and safety; do not misrepresent credentials or use them
improperly; **maintain competence through continuing education**; and comply with laws and
BCSP requirements.

**The paramount duty is people's safety and health.** When a question pits employer loyalty,
cost, or schedule against a clear danger to workers, safety wins — and the correct action is
usually to **document the hazard in writing and escalate through the chain**, not to go
public first and not to stay quiet. Whistleblowing is the last step, not the first.

**Professional practice cautions:**

- Don't sign off on something you didn't verify
- Don't let a title imply expertise you don't have
- Don't perform tests, calibrations, or interpretations outside your training
- Document your recommendations and their disposition — including when management declines
  them. That record protects the workers, the company, and you
- Report accurately; falsifying an OSHA log is a federal offense, and pressure to
  under-record is an ethics problem before it's a legal one

## 7. Worker impairment

**Sources of impairment:** alcohol · illegal drugs · **prescription and over-the-counter
medications** · **cannabis** · **fatigue** · acute stress, grief, and mental health
conditions · medical conditions (uncontrolled diabetes, seizure disorders, sleep apnea) ·
extreme heat or cold.

**Fatigue is impairment.** ~17 hours awake ≈ 0.05% BAC; ~24 hours ≈ 0.10%. Treat fatigue risk
management as a safety program element, not a personal problem.

**Drug and alcohol testing:**

- **DOT-regulated testing** (49 CFR Part 40) is prescriptive: pre-employment, random,
  reasonable suspicion, post-accident, return-to-duty, and follow-up; specified panel;
  **split specimen**; **Medical Review Officer** review of all non-negative results; strict
  chain of custody
- **Non-DOT testing** is governed by state law, which varies widely. Some states restrict
  random testing, require written policies, or protect medical cannabis users
- **OSHA's position (1904.35)**: **blanket post-incident drug testing that would deter
  reporting is prohibited** as retaliatory. Post-incident testing is permissible when there
  is a **reasonable possibility that drug use contributed** to the incident, when required by
  another law (DOT, state workers' comp premium discount programs), or as part of a broader
  investigation of all contributing factors. **Testing everyone who reports an injury,
  regardless of plausibility, is the violation**
- **Cannabis** — legal in many states, still **Schedule I federally**. THC tests do not
  measure current impairment (metabolites persist for weeks), which is the core problem for
  safety-sensitive positions. Impairment-based assessment and fitness-for-duty evaluation
  are the emerging approach. **Never** treat a positive THC test alone as proof of on-the-job
  impairment
- **Alcohol** — measured by breath or blood, and does correlate with current impairment

**Reasonable suspicion** must be based on **specific, articulable, contemporaneous
observations** of appearance, behavior, speech, or odor — documented, and ideally confirmed
by two trained supervisors. Supervisor training on recognition is a program requirement.

**Employee Assistance Programs (EAPs)** and a treatment-not-discipline approach for substance
use disorders; the **ADA protects individuals in recovery** and those with a history of
addiction, but **not** current illegal drug use. Alcoholism is a protected disability; being
drunk at work is not protected conduct.
